# What Vera can learn from a metaphor

These six invented cases show how an image can suggest a question. The numerical examples can be checked under the stated assumptions. The studies involving people are proposals. Their outcomes are open.

[Learning procedure](metaphor-learning.md) · [Deutsch](../metaphern-atlas.md)

## 1. A bridge between two meanings

A footbridge connects two banks. At a town meeting two people call for “free access.” One means that nobody should have to pay. The other means that anyone should be able to walk in without registering. Bridging the gap would give each person a way to understand what the other means.

**Test.** Give both people a new case, such as an event that charges nothing but requires advance registration. Can each predict how the other would judge access? The other person confirms or corrects the account. To test whether the bridge metaphor itself helps, randomly assign groups to receive either the metaphor or an equally clear literal explanation and try fresh cases.

**Where it breaks.** Both people might understand each other perfectly and still want different rules. Agreement would be the wrong measure of understanding. Each is free to stay on their own bank.

## 2. A buffer at handover

A rain barrel has six litres of room before a shower. If ten litres flow in and none drain away during that time, the barrel can hold six of them. A schedule can absorb a delay in much the same way. A care handover is set for 3 pm. Before the next fixed appointment, the schedule allows ten minutes more than the handover usually takes.

**Test.** If the handover takes its usual time, arriving seven minutes late leaves the next appointment on time. Arriving fourteen minutes late sets it back by four. For the next handover, check how much time is actually left.

**Where it breaks.** If the person receiving care needs longer to feel comfortable with someone that day, the handover itself takes longer. The buffer describes the schedule. How much time someone needs is a question in its own right.

## 3. What feedback tells you

A violinist tunes a string to a reference pitch. Hearing that the string is flat tells her which way to adjust it. An apprentice planing a wooden tenon can do more with “one millimetre too wide” on the next piece than with “it doesn't fit yet.” Both kinds of feedback identify the direction of an error.

**Test.** Randomly divide learners into two groups. Both plane several practice pieces with the same tool. After each attempt the first group hears whether the piece fits. The second also hears how far the tenon is over or under the target width. Give both groups a new width for the final piece and no feedback. The prediction is that the second group will come closer on average. Measure the distance from the target in millimetres, counting errors in either direction.

**Where it breaks.** If the gauge reads one millimetre high, practice might produce increasingly consistent tenons that are all too narrow. Check the work against an independently verified gauge to see what the learners actually learned.

## 4. Two routes with a common point of failure

On a habitat map, two wildlife trails lead from a patch of woodland to a meadow. Both cross the road through the same culvert. A community library keeps two copies of its catalogue on separate drives in the same room. Under a particular failure, the two trails share a dependency, as do the two copies.

**Test.** Close the culvert on the map and try tracing either marked trail to the meadow. The corresponding test at the library is to restore the catalogue while the whole room is inaccessible. A readable copy at another accessible site changes the outcome.

**Where it breaks.** If a single drive fails, a second copy in the same room is sufficient. Out in the landscape, a crossing might exist that nobody has mapped yet. A judgment about resilience depends both on the specified failure and on how complete the map is.

## 5. What the scale leaves out

On a street map a flight of steps can disappear into the symbol for a path. For a wheelchair user a single step might decide whether the whole route works. In an annual summary a week without income can disappear into a total. Both depictions can be accurate for their purpose and still leave a crucial question open.

**Test.** Two households start with empty accounts. Each receives 200 euros over two weeks. Household A gets the money on day one and household B on the final day. Both owe 100 euros on day seven. Without credit or other funds, only A can pay then. The map suggests a question about the accounts. How fine a timescale would show the difference?

**Where it breaks.** The total is sufficient to answer a question about total income. The decision at hand determines which detail matters.

## 6. When someone is missing at the table

“Since you've been gone, silence sits at the table with me.” The line gives an absence a seat. Music can invite a similar experience by leaving out a note the listener has come to expect. When a community announces a minute of silence, everyone present may know the occasion. What someone feels during it can differ greatly from what an observer imagines.

**Test.** Vera might initially read the line as grief and ask, “What's that silence like for you?” The speaker might reply, “It's a relief. For once nobody is interrogating me.” Vera would then need to revise her reading. Without an answer, the interpretation stays open.

**Where it breaks.** The image alone cannot establish grief or predict how it will unfold. A poem can sustain several readings. In conversation the speaker can say which reading does justice to their experience.

## What counts as learning

A metaphor can bring a question within reach that Vera had overlooked. At the library, what counts is a copy that can be restored. At the town meeting, it is an account that the person whose view it describes recognises as accurate. At the table, it is whether Vera revises a hasty reading in response to what the speaker says.

Anyone who knows these six cases can answer from memory. A further trial needs fresh tasks, a comparison answer without the proposed transfer, and cases in which applying the metaphor should fail. Similar images inherited from one tradition can retell the same idea. Independent evidence needs a separate origin.
